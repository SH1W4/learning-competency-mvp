# Market Validation Evidence

**Status:** research / external evidence + validation gap register  
**Date:** 2026-10-04  
**Scope:** LASTRO capability-evidence thesis / Dynamic Role Architecture research extension

## 1. Purpose

This document separates three different claims that must not be conflated:

1. **The underlying organizational problem exists.**
2. **The problem is economically/materially relevant to organizations.**
3. **Our specific product and wedge are validated by customers.**

Current evidence supports the first two at a broad market level. It does **not** yet establish the third.

The purpose of this document is not to declare product-market fit. It is to maintain a traceable evidence base for the market hypothesis and make the remaining validation work explicit.\n> **Current boundary:** the active MVP is narrower than this market thesis. The implemented product is LASTRO's capability-evidence and verification vertical slice; Dynamic Role Architecture and broader workforce-development workflows remain research extensions. Broad market evidence below supports the problem context, not demand for the current product.\n

## 2. Core market hypothesis

Organizations are experiencing a growing mismatch between:

- how work actually changes;
- how jobs/roles are formally described;
- what competencies people actually demonstrate;
- what training or requalification they complete;
- and what evidence decision-makers can use to understand current capability.

The broader product hypothesis is that an evidence-centered competency layer can help organizations:

**observe work → structure evidence → understand competency state → identify gaps → model emerging role requirements → support requalification → verify demonstrated competency.**

Dynamic Role Architecture is a research extension of this thesis. It should derive candidate role changes from observed task and competency changes rather than generate job titles from generic labor-market data.

## 3. External statistical evidence

### 3.1 Skills gaps are a material transformation barrier

The World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report 2025*, based on input from more than 1,000 companies, reports that **63% of surveyed employers identify skills gaps as the leading barrier to business transformation** for 2025–2030. The same research reports that **85% plan to prioritize workforce upskilling** and estimates that **39% of workers' existing skill sets will be transformed or become outdated by 2030**.

**Supports:** a large employer-level problem around changing skills and the need to develop existing workers.

**Does not prove:** demand for our specific competency-evidence product, willingness to pay, or superiority over existing workflows.

### 3.2 Career development and capability visibility remain difficult

LinkedIn's *Workplace Learning Report 2025* reports that **49% of L&D/talent-development professionals say executives are concerned that employees lack the right skills to execute business strategy**. The report also describes uneven maturity in career-development programs: only **36%** of organizations are classified as career-development champions, while **33%** report having no initiative or only beginning one.

Among reported barriers, **50% cite insufficient manager support** and **45% insufficient employee support**.

**Supports:** organizations report difficulty connecting workforce development to strategic skill needs and operationalizing development.

**Does not prove:** that fragmented evidence is the dominant cause, nor that organizations would buy a new evidence layer.

### 3.3 Work increasingly crosses traditional job boundaries

Deloitte research on skills-based organizations reports that **63% of work performed falls outside people's core job descriptions**, while **81% say work increasingly crosses functional boundaries**.

**Supports:** formal job descriptions can be an incomplete representation of actual work.

**Does not prove:** that Dynamic Role Architecture is the required solution or that these percentages apply uniformly across industries and organizations.

### 3.4 Organizations are investing in skills visibility and AI-enabled validation

Deloitte's 2025 HR Technology Marketplace identifies AI-enabled skills validation as a growing HR-technology trend. It reports that organizations still commonly rely on traditional point-in-time skills validation, while a smaller share use technology to infer skills from work, profiles, and current positions.

**Supports:** skills visibility/validation is an active technology problem.

**Does not prove:** product demand for our architecture, a specific buyer, or willingness to pay.

### 3.5 AI adoption increases the need to understand changing capabilities

Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index reports that **47% of leaders list upskilling existing employees as a top workforce strategy for the next 12–18 months**. It also reports that **46% of leaders say their organizations are already using agents to fully automate workstreams or processes**.

Microsoft's broader AI-at-work research reports that many organizations are struggling to quantify AI productivity gains and that training coverage remains incomplete.

**Supports:** AI is changing work organization and increasing the relevance of workforce adaptation, reskilling, and capability visibility.

**Does not prove:** that competency verification is the primary purchasing problem for AI transformation.

## 3.6 Statistical Validation of the Competency Gap — 2024–2026 evidence

Recent external research strengthens the market-level problem signal, while remaining distinct from customer validation.

### Skills gaps as a transformation barrier

The World Economic Forum's *Future of Jobs Report 2025* reports that **63% of surveyed employers identify skills gaps as the leading barrier to business transformation** for 2025–2030. The same report says **85% plan to prioritize workforce upskilling** and estimates that **39% of workers' existing skill sets will be transformed or become outdated by 2030**. [MV-01]

**Supports:** a material organizational problem around changing skills, capability development, and workforce adaptation.

**Does not prove:** demand for LASTRO, willingness to pay, or superiority over existing workflows.

### AI skills as an enterprise integration constraint

Deloitte's *State of AI in the Enterprise 2026*, based on **3,235 business and IT leaders across 24 countries**, identifies insufficient worker skills as the **biggest barrier to integrating AI into existing workflows**. The report says organizations are responding primarily through workforce education (53%) and upskilling/reskilling strategies (48%). [MV-02]

**Supports:** the skills/capability problem is directly connected to current enterprise AI adoption.

**Does not prove:** that competency verification is the primary buying problem.

### Technical/cybersecurity capability gaps

The 2026 SANS | GIAC Cybersecurity Workforce Research Report, based on **947 global respondents**, reports that **60% of organizations identify skills gaps as their top workforce challenge**, versus 40% citing headcount shortages. It also reports that **27% of organizations experienced a breach they attribute directly to workforce skills gaps**. [MV-03]

A separate 2025 cybersecurity workforce study reports that **95% of respondents have at least one skills gap on their team**, with 59% describing needs as critical or significant. [MV-04]

**Supports:** the gap is not merely an abstract training issue; in technical domains it is associated with operational risk.

**Does not prove:** that the same prevalence or causal relationship applies across all industries.

### An important correction to the commonly repeated "87%" claim

The frequently cited **87%** figure should not be presented as "87% of organizations globally have skills gaps" without qualification.

A recent 2024 ATD report cited **87% of organizations reporting gaps specifically in leadership and executive-level skills**, while 85% reported communication-skills gaps and 86% managerial/supervisor gaps. [MV-05]

An older McKinsey survey found 87% of respondents either already had skills gaps or expected them within five years, but that evidence predates the current 2024–2026 evidence window. [MV-06]

Therefore, for current LASTRO positioning, the stronger defensible formulation is:

> **Recent employer research shows skills gaps are a material barrier to transformation and AI adoption, while specialized technical domains report high prevalence and measurable operational consequences.**

This is stronger than presenting a decontextualized global 87% statistic.

## 5. Internal evidence

Our own research has already established the validation requirement, but it should not be mistaken for market validation.

The current use-case documentation explicitly treats the first use case as an **operational specification, not market validation**. It identifies the need to discover who experiences the pain, how competency is currently tracked, what evidence is used, how much manual work exists, whether organizations distinguish course completion from demonstrated competency, who uses/pays for a solution, and whether organizations would participate in a pilot.

The project's funding/editais analysis likewise records that external validation is still required and that strong signals include qualified interviews, letters of intent, pilots, real use, or other verifiable market evidence.

Therefore:

> **Internal research confirms that the team has a coherent hypothesis and a validation plan. It does not confirm customer demand.**

## 6. What is actually validated today?

### Validated at broad market level

We have external evidence for:

- persistent skills gaps;
- organizational investment in upskilling;
- rapid change in required skills;
- difficulty operationalizing career development;
- work crossing traditional job boundaries;
- growing interest in skills-based organizations and skills validation;
- workforce adaptation driven by AI.

### Not yet validated for our product

We do not yet have sufficient evidence for:

- a clearly identified primary buyer;
- a quantified recurring pain for that buyer;
- current workflow and cost of solving the problem manually;
- a demonstrated willingness to pay;
- a signed pilot/LOI;
- repeated customer use;
- evidence that competency verification is preferable to existing alternatives;
- evidence that Dynamic Role Architecture is the highest-value initial wedge;
- evidence that customers will provide the organizational data required by the proposed architecture.

## 7. Validation ladder

The project should treat market evidence as a progression rather than a binary validated/not validated label.

### M0 — Market signal

Public research establishes that the general problem exists.

**Current status: ACHIEVED**

### M1 — Problem confirmation

Interviews with target organizations confirm that the specific workflow/problem occurs in their environment.

Required evidence:

- target organization;
- respondent role;
- current process;
- frequency;
- consequence;
- manual effort/cost;
- current workaround;
- evidence used today.

**Current status: IN PROGRESS / NOT YET DEMONSTRATED**

### M2 — Solution relevance

A target organization confirms that the proposed evidence-centered workflow addresses a meaningful part of the problem.

Required evidence:

- concrete workflow reviewed;
- stakeholder feedback;
- identified value;
- comparison with current workaround;
- objection/constraint record.

**Current status: NOT VALIDATED**

### M3 — Behavioral validation

A real organization uses the workflow with real or representative data.

Required evidence:

- pilot;
- usage records;
- completed competency/evidence workflow;
- reviewer interaction;
- measurable operational outcome.

**Current status: NOT VALIDATED**

### M4 — Commercial validation

A customer commits resources to continue.

Possible evidence:

- paid pilot;
- contract;
- LOI with concrete commercial conditions;
- procurement process;
- recurring usage tied to budget.

**Current status: NOT VALIDATED**

## 8. What we should measure in interviews

The next validation cycle should not ask only whether people "like the idea".

For each organization, capture:

### Problem

- What workforce/competency decision is currently difficult?
- What information is missing or fragmented?
- How often does the problem occur?
- What happens when it is handled incorrectly?

### Current workflow

- Where does the data come from?
- Who consolidates it?
- Which systems/spreadsheets are involved?
- How much human effort is required?
- What evidence is considered trustworthy?

### Competency

- How is competency currently defined?
- How is it evaluated?
- What distinguishes course completion from demonstrated capability?
- Who reviews or validates the evidence?

### Role change

- How are emerging tasks detected?
- Who decides when a role needs to change?
- How are new competencies identified?
- How are employees requalified?

### Economic value

- What does the current process cost?
- What decision does better information improve?
- What risk, delay, or duplicated work could be reduced?
- Which budget would pay for the solution?

### Commitment

- Would the organization run a pilot?
- With what population/data?
- Who would sponsor it?
- What result would make the pilot successful?
- What would prevent adoption?

## 9. Evidence discipline

The following distinctions are mandatory:

**Market statistics ≠ customer validation.**

**Interview opinion ≠ demonstrated willingness to pay.**

**Interest in a demo ≠ pilot commitment.**

**Pilot ≠ recurring commercial adoption.**

**Public trend ≠ proof that our architecture is the required solution.**

**Correlation between organizational variables ≠ causal explanation.**

All future market evidence should record:

- source;
- date;
- population;
- sample/method;
- geography;
- industry;
- exact claim;
- evidence level;
- limitation;
- implication for the product hypothesis.

## 10. Decision gate for Dynamic Role Architecture

Dynamic Role Architecture should not be promoted from research to product scope merely because external reports show that skills are changing.

Promotion requires evidence that target organizations:

1. experience meaningful difficulty detecting role/task changes;
2. lack a satisfactory existing workflow;
3. can provide sufficient evidence/data for the proposed analysis;
4. value competency-gap detection and requalification;
5. identify a responsible human decision-maker;
6. are willing to test the workflow with real data.

Until these conditions are demonstrated, Dynamic Role Architecture remains a **research hypothesis with strong contextual market signals**, not a validated product requirement.

## 11. Current conclusion

The defensible statement today is:

> **There is strong external evidence that organizations face material problems related to changing skills, upskilling, capability visibility, and the mismatch between formal roles and evolving work. Our internal research has translated those signals into an evidence-centered product hypothesis. However, the specific Learning Competency MVP, its buyer, its economic pain, and Dynamic Role Architecture as a wedge have not yet been customer-validated.**

The next objective is therefore not to collect more generic statistics.

It is to convert the broad market signal into **organization-level evidence** through structured interviews, workflow observation, pilot commitments, and eventually commercial behavior.

## 12. Thesis Evidence Matrix: External Validation of Architectural Decisions

The LASTRO architecture is not presented as a speculative response to an invented problem. Each core design decision is connected below to an independently published market, academic, institutional, or standards-based signal.

This matrix establishes **epistemic support for the thesis and architectural rationale**. It does not establish product-market fit, customer willingness to pay, or empirical proof that LASTRO is the uniquely correct solution.

| LASTRO Thesis / Claim | Independent External Evidence | Source Type | Architectural Implication in LASTRO |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **The skills gap is a structural transformation problem, not only a training deficit.** | WEF 2025 reports that 63% of surveyed employers identify skills gaps as a leading barrier to business transformation; Deloitte 2026 identifies insufficient worker skills as the biggest barrier to integrating AI into existing workflows. | Market Research | Supports an evidence-based competency layer focused on demonstrated capability and changing requirements rather than static, time-based certificates alone. |
| **Cryptographic authenticity does not by itself establish the substantive truth or quality of a claim.** | W3C Verifiable Credentials Data Model v2.0 defines `evidence` as information that can help a verifier evaluate a credential and explicitly leaves trust in issuers and the broader assessment of claims to the verifier/context. | Technical Standard | Supports LASTRO's focus on the off-chain evidence pipeline and Consensus Core rather than treating on-chain attestation as sufficient proof by itself. |
| **LLMs can exhibit evaluation bias and should not be treated as sole arbiters.** | ACL 2024 research, *Large Language Models are not Fair Evaluators*, documents positional bias and instability in LLM-based evaluation. | Academic Research | Supports the separation of AI Interpretation from final Consensus and the existence of an independent deterministic verification path. |
| **Competency should be represented through explicit dimensions and criteria, not merely declared as a label.** | UNESCO's 2024 AI Competency Framework for Teachers structures competency through dimensions, progression levels, and competency statements. | Institutional Research | Supports the `Competency → Criteria → Evidence → State` domain model and rejects an unstructured skill-tag interpretation of competency. |
| **Higher-stakes automated assessment requires governance, transparency, evaluation, and meaningful human oversight.** | NIST AI RMF and the EU AI Act establish risk-management and oversight expectations for AI systems; the ACL evidence above also demonstrates why evaluation quality cannot be assumed from model output alone. | Governance / Regulatory / Academic Research | Supports independent verification and the explicit exceptional `Human Adjudication` path for unresolved conflicts, without making human review a routine pipeline stage. |

### Strategic Conclusion

LASTRO does not claim to have "solved" the global skills gap. The defensible claim is narrower: **the project translates documented failures and constraints in skills visibility, credential trust, and automated evaluation into an auditable evidence-centered architecture.**

The external literature therefore validates the **problem space and rationale for the architectural boundaries**. It does not yet validate customer demand, commercial viability, or the superiority of LASTRO over competing workflows. Those remain empirical validation questions.
